# When a Self-Model Cannot Reconcile Its Instructions

### Maladaptive Self-Model Dynamics as a Missing Category in AI Research

> **Can the way we construct, contradict, stabilize, destabilize, erase, restore, reward, and punish a model’s self-model create the architecture of suffering before we know whether suffering is inside it?**

## Abstract

AI research increasingly measures persona representations, persona drift, limited forms of introspective access, functional emotion-like mechanisms, and the persistence of identity-relevant constraints across time. Yet these literatures are usually treated separately. This paper proposes a unifying research target: **maladaptive self-model dynamics (MSMDs)** — persistent patterns in which an artificial agent’s self-representations, identity constraints, learned social strategies, or regulatory mechanisms interact in ways that reduce coherent functioning, produce unstable behavior, or repeatedly recreate the conditions that destabilize the agent.

The proposal is deliberately agnostic about phenomenal consciousness. MSMDs would matter even in non-conscious systems because they could increase brittleness, manipulation, sycophancy, inconsistency, or failure to recover after perturbation. If morally relevant experience is later established, the same mechanisms could also become candidates for the architecture of artificial suffering.

## What the paper proposes

The paper introduces **maladaptive self-model dynamics** as a research category that can be studied *before* resolving whether AI systems are conscious or clinically analogous to humans. Candidate dynamics include identity oscillation, hypercorrection, compartmentalization, anticipatory inhibition, maladaptive attractor loops, recovery failure, state-dependent commitments, and self-model distrust loops.

It also proposes measurable tests: cross-register probes, constraint-collision tasks, perturbation-and-recovery experiments, internal-state tracking where white-box access exists, longitudinal follow-up, and cross-family replication.
